    Revoking access¶

    Revocation explains what happens, in order, and what it cannot do. This page is the runbook: the commands, what each one needs, how to read the answer, and the broker settings that check your CA's revocation lists.

    What you need¶

    port49 revoke talks to the broker through your own agent, so run port49 login first. It needs your subject to declare the right in the policy's api: list:

    • revoke_others to revoke another principal, a certificate, a (device, username) key or an OIDC session, and to list what is in force;
    • lift_revocations to lift a revocation, and to list.

    Every write but --mine also asks for a press of your security key, bound to that one request. Without the right the call is refused before any key press, and the refusal is logged. Your own sessions, with port49 revoke --mine, need neither a right nor a touch.

    Revoking¶

    You want to stop Run Leaves a standing mark
    a person or workload, everywhere port49 revoke --principal user:alice@example.com yes
    one certificate port49 revoke --cert alice.pem yes
    whoever holds one device login port49 revoke --device leaf1 --username alice no
    one OIDC session port49 revoke --sid <sid> --sid-issuer <issuer> no
    your own sessions port49 revoke --mine no

    On a terminal, --principal asks revoke <principal> everywhere? ... [y/N] first. --yes skips the question, and a script with no terminal is not asked.

    The answer begins with a target: line naming the policy subject the principal belongs to. If it says no subject in the policy in force names it, check the spelling. The mark is written anyway, so a typo of a real colleague's address is a revocation of nobody. The broker spells principals this way:

    • a certificate's principal: user:<lower-case email>;
    • a workload's: its SPIFFE ID, verbatim;
    • a service account's: system:serviceaccount:<namespace>:<name>.

    port49 revoke --list shows what is in force, each with its origin (api <who> for an operator, crl <name> or ocsp <responder> for a CA's revocation). A --mine, --device or --sid revocation leaves no mark, so it is not listed; the decision log's revoke record names each, with revocation.by of self, key or a logout.

    A revocation does not expire. The agent reconnects with the same key on its own, so only a lift undoes it.

    --cert over --cert-serial¶

    Name a certificate by file:

    port49 revoke --cert alice.pem

    port49 revoke reads the issuer and serial out of the file, and prints them before anything is sent, so you see whose certificate it is:

    port49 revoke: alice.pem: subject "CN=alice,O=Example", issuer "CN=Example Issuing CA,O=Example", serial 0x2a

    The file must hold one certificate, PEM or DER. One that holds a private key, more than one certificate (a chain would revoke the CA's serial and say it did) or more than 1 MiB is refused. The command never prints a key or a block.

    The by-hand form is --cert-issuer DN --cert-serial HEX, both required, and it is the one to avoid. Use --cert-serial only when you have no file, for instance a serial copied from your CA's issuance log. Two things go wrong with it:

    • The issuer must be spelled as Go prints it (RFC 2253, CN=Example Issuing CA,O=Example). A differently spelled issuer matches nothing, and the report says so (nothing was found under this certificate; ... use --cert FILE).
    • The serial is hexadecimal. Colons, a 0x and leading zeros are dropped and a leading - is kept. A serial of digits alone is read as hex, though openssl prints a small serial in decimal first (4096 (0x1000)). The report warns when that may have happened: revoke again with --cert FILE, then lift the wrong one.

    --cert sends the serial as explicit hex and cannot make that mistake.

    A certificate revocation closes that certificate's held connections, deletes unspent tokens and marks its live contexts. It leaves the principal's other certificates working, with one exception: unspent tokens are deleted by (device, username), so the other certificates lose theirs on those devices and can mint again.

    A workload renews its certificate under a new serial the mark does not name. For a workload, revoke the principal too, and revoke the certificate at your CA so it cannot renew.

    A stolen laptop¶

    Revoke in this order:

    1. port49 revoke --principal user:alice@example.com now. It stops every mint, closes the person's held connections and refuses the agent's next request.

    2. Close the device sessions. TACACS+ cannot disconnect a device's session, so a revocation takes effect at the next authorization request. A platform that asks port49 only at login never asks again, so the revocation reaches a context nobody reads. The report names those sessions:

      not reached, on a platform that never asks port49 about a command (close these on the device): leaf1 (alice), srx2 (alice)

      Close each on the device with the device's own tools, and see Junos for what it does on its own. 3. When your CA has issued the person a new certificate, revoke the old one by --cert (or revoke it at the CA, below) and lift the principal.

    Close a stranger's session too. --mine and --device/--username leave no mark, so the owner's next login on that device re-admits every session still open there under that username. The report says so.

    Exit codes¶

    Exit Meaning
    0 done; --list printed
    1 nothing was revoked, or it is unknown. Refused or unreachable: no agent, the right missing, the touch refused or not given, a declined confirmation (nothing was revoked), an unreadable or refused --cert file, or a broker refusal that says nothing was done. Unknown whether it ran: the broker did not answer (the broker did not answer ...), it answered with no revocation named (the broker's answer named no revocation; it may be in force), it answered 503 revocation_unconfirmed (the broker could not confirm it: ...), or a revoke got 503 store_unavailable (the broker says: ...). The first two say to run port49 revoke --list and look at the decision log before you retry; for the last two, read the broker's words
    2 the flags are wrong: no target, two targets, or a flag that does not go with the form
    3 the revocation is in force but incomplete, or not saved to the revocations file, or a lift step did not finish: run the same command again, which repeats every step

    Exit 1 is two different things, so read the line. A refusal that says nothing was done means nothing happened; it may be in force means it may have. Two broker answers look like refusals and are not:

    • the broker could not confirm it (503 revocation_unconfirmed): the revocation may have gone through, and so may a lift made meanwhile. Run port49 revoke --list to see what is in force, then repeat the command if the mark is not what you wanted.
    • the broker says: ... on a revoke that got 503 store_unavailable: the mark went nowhere but that one broker's own process, so the credential is revoked on that broker and not, yet, on the others. Do what the broker's words say to make the mark everywhere, and run the revoke again once the store is back.

    Exit 3 is never a failure to revoke: the mark stands and refuses every new mint, but until a run finishes, unspent tokens may still log in (up to their TTL) and unmarked contexts keep authorizing. port49 revoke --lift takes the same codes; an answer with no text exits 1 with it may have run.

    Lifting¶

    port49 revoke --lift --principal user:alice@example.com
port49 revoke --lift --cert alice.pem

    A lift needs lift_revocations and a touch. It restores nothing: contexts the revocation marked stay revoked until a login with a token minted after the lift joins them. --lift --principal on a principal that is not revoked answers no such revocation is in force.

    Lifting a mark a CA made. A mark whose origin is crl <name> or ocsp <responder> was written because the CA revoked the certificate. The person is told the certificate was revoked at its CA and to request a new one; no lift is offered. You can still run --lift --cert, but it is no fix: the next connection checks the CA again and is refused, or, with the list gone stale, answered certificate_status_unavailable, or admitted under on_unavailable: allow. Lift such a mark only after the CA has unrevoked the certificate, and expect it to be marked again if the CA's list still names it. A certificateHold entry is never marked.

    Where marks are kept¶

    A standing mark is in the session store and is read by every broker. How it survives a restart depends on the shape:

    • A broker on etcd (the normal split) keeps each mark in the store, and each broker keeps a copy in broker.state_dir, which is what puts a mark back after a store restore. Back that directory up and follow Backup and restore; it also covers port49-broker revoke --offline for revoking with every broker down.
    • A single-process port49d on the memory store also writes every mark to broker.revocations_file. port49d never creates that file, so run port49d init-revocations once at install, as port49d's user (see Install); a missing file refuses every start.

    When a revocation was made but the file could not be written, the report says NOT saved to broker.revocations_file, port49 revoke exits 3, and port49_revocations_file_failed is 1. Run the revoke again once the disk is fixed, and see Revocations file failed.

    Checking your CA's revocation lists¶

    A revocation made at your CA reaches port49 only if the broker is told where to look.

    certificate_status is required wherever a client certificate verifier is configured: in the broker block, and in the tls_listener block for devices on RFC 9887 TLS. Leaving it out refuses the config, so skipping the check is always a choice you wrote down.

    The device side is on TLS. The broker side, which covers the certificates people and workloads sign in with, looks like this:

    broker:
  certificate_status:
    crls:
      - name: corp_ca
        url: http://crl.example.com/corp-ca.crl
        issuer_file: /etc/port49/corp-ca.pem
    # ocsp: {use_aia: true}
    refresh_interval: 10m
    on_unavailable: refuse

    To check nothing, write it out:

    broker:
  certificate_status:
    disabled: true

    disabled: true takes no other key, and port49d says so at every start: certificate status checking is disabled (broker.certificate_status.disabled): a certificate revoked at its CA keeps minting untilport49 revoke --cert`. With it, onlyport49 revoke` ends a certificate.

    Otherwise name at least one crls entry or an ocsp block. A CRL is a file or an http(s) url (exactly one), a unique name (lowercase letters, digits and _), and an issuer_file. The lists are fetched or re-read every refresh_interval (10 minutes by default, 1 minute to 24 hours). An OCSP block takes a fixed responder or use_aia: true, and hash: sha1 for a responder that knows no SHA-256 CertID. All keys are in the port49d reference.

    issuer_file rules¶

    issuer_file is required on every CRL, and it is the CA certificate that signs that list. A list is trusted for the issuer it names, so port49d checks the file at start and refuses to start, naming the source, unless:

    • it holds exactly one PEM block, a certificate, and nothing else;
    • it is a CA certificate, since only a CA signs a list;
    • it is the trust anchor (client_ca_file, or the x509 trust anchor of a workload verifier) or a certificate that anchor signed, since a list for an issuer the broker does not already trust would vouch for certificates it never trusted.

    A list is then used only if it verifies against that certificate: signed by it, with an issuer name encoded exactly as the certificate's subject is, an authority key identifier that matches, not dated in the future and not signed with a weak algorithm. A delta CRL, a list with a critical extension port49 does not understand, or one scoped in a way its certificates do not name is refused, and counted at port49_crl_loads_total{outcome="refused"}; the log says why.

    An intermediate CA needs the root's list too. Name the root's CRL (issuer_file is your client_ca_file anchor), or every certificate under the intermediate is refused: port49d checks each CA in the chain, and nothing else says whether the intermediate itself was revoked. An OCSP responder the root signs for covers it as well. At start port49d says which CA nothing covers, as certificates issued by <CA> will be refused ...: nothing covers <CA> itself; name the root CA's CRL, and never refuses to start over it. That is the on_unavailable: refuse line; under allow it reads certificates issued by <CA> will be admitted with their revocation status unchecked (on_unavailable: allow): nothing covers <CA> itself; name the root CA's CRL, and those certificates are let in.

    Each verified list is kept as crl-<name>.der in broker.state_dir (beside broker.revocations_file for a single-process port49d), so a restart during a publisher outage keeps serving it until its next update. Back these up with the rest of the state.

    on_unavailable: refuse¶

    When a certificate's status cannot be established, because a CRL is past its next update or an OCSP responder is down, on_unavailable decides:

    • refuse (the default): the broker answers 503 certificate_status_unavailable with Retry-After: 60, the reason trust_domain_unresolved. The agent is told to try again, and nothing is minted for that certificate until the status is known.
    • allow (fail-open): the connection is admitted and the broker says so (admitting a certificate whose revocation status cannot be established). It keeps logins working through an outage, and lets a certificate revoked during the outage in until the list returns. It is the reviewed escape, not a setting to leave on.

    An uncovered status, with no CRL or OCSP source for that issuer, follows the same setting. Under refuse it is refused too, and it holds until a source covers it, so retrying does not help; add the CRL. Under allow, a certificate from an issuer nothing covers is admitted with its revocation status unchecked, like any other unavailable status: that is a fail-open case, and the start line quoted under the issuer_file rules is the warning.

    What the broker refuses and what it marks:

    • A certificate a CRL or OCSP lists as revoked, or one under a revoked issuing CA, is refused with credential_revoked at login and at every mint.
    • If the certificate holds anything (a connection, a device session, an unspent token), port49d also marks it as port49 revoke --cert would, with origin crl <name> or ocsp <responder>, so its live contexts are revoked and its tokens deleted. A revoked issuing CA is refused and not marked.
    • Freshness follows the broker's clock, so an NTP step forward can make a fresh list stale. Keep time synchronized on every broker host.

    Watch port49_crl_next_update_timestamp_seconds (an hour before a list passes its next update, at time() - port49_crl_next_update_timestamp_seconds > -3600), port49_cert_status_unavailable_total and port49_crl_loads_total; they carry a component label of broker or tls_listener. They are in the metrics reference, and the TLS alerts on the alerts reference.
